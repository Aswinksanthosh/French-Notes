### Grammar: Accents

- French uses accent marks that change pronunciation.
- Accent aigu (é) — acute accent.
- Accent grave (è) — grave accent.
- Cédille (ç) — turns a "c" before a/o/u into a soft "s" sound instead of a hard "k" sound.
- Tréma (ï) — shows two vowels next to each other are pronounced separately, not blended into one sound.

- français — the cédille makes this "franss-ay", not "frank-ay".
- François — a common first name using the same cédille.
- maïs (corn) vs mais (but) — the tréma on maïs keeps the "a" and "i" as two separate sounds; without it, "mais" is one blended sound meaning "but".

### Pronunciation practice: flagged letters

- Letters flagged in class for extra pronunciation practice: D, E, G, J, Q, R, U, V, W, Y.
- E — a drawn-out "euh" sound.
- R — the guttural French R; compared in class to the "r" sound in "Air India".

---

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Accent aigu vs accent grave, in more depth:** accent aigu (é) is a rising "ay" sound; accent grave (è) is a flatter, more open "eh" sound — the two accents exist specifically to distinguish those two E sounds in writing.
- **Accent circonflexe (ê/â/ô/î/û):** a third accent, not covered in the printed notes. Historically many circumflexed words used to be spelled with an "s" that was later dropped (e.g. hôpital used to be "hospital" — the accent is a trace of that lost letter). It can sit on a, e, i, o, or u. Examples given in class: hôpital (hospital), j'ai hâte de te voir ("I can't wait to see you" — hâte = haste), île (island).
- **Cédille, why it exists:** without it, "c" before a/o/u is a hard "k" sound. Adding the cédille (ç) softens it to "s". Example walked through live: françois written without the cédille would sound like "frank-wa"; with it, français correctly sounds like "fran-say". It's only ever used with the letter C.
- **Tréma, which vowels it applies to:** a student asked directly which letters can take a tréma — the instructor confirmed only O, E, and I (not A or U), and specifically it goes on the *second* of two vowels sitting next to each other, to show they're pronounced separately rather than blended into one sound.
- **Tréma example drilled in class:** maïs (corn) vs mais (but) — removing the tréma from maïs changes both the pronunciation and the meaning of the word, which is why the instructor called accents "very important", not decorative.
- **Student question on E vs U:** a student asked how to tell the E sound and the U sound apart, since they can sound similar to a beginner's ear — the instructor worked through it live using the word "élément" to isolate the E sound by contrast.

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Why vous is "formal":** vous is grammatically a plural form, even when addressing a single person — using the plural on purpose, as a sign of distance/respect, is literally what makes it formal. tu is reserved for people you know well (friends, family) and is considered too familiar for a stranger or a boss.
- **Liaison, explained through vous/nous:** the final "s" on vous or nous is normally silent, but when the next word starts with a vowel, that "s" is pronounced as a "z" sound linking the two words together — vous allez is said as "vou-zallez", nous allons as "nou-zallons". Without a following vowel, the s stays silent (vous, nous on their own).
- **Gender rule for groups (il vs elle in the plural):** if a group has even one male member, the whole group is referred to with ils (masculine plural) — no matter how many females are in the group or how few males. Only an all-female group takes elles. The instructor explicitly flagged this as a quirk English speakers find surprising, since English "they" doesn't carry gender at all.
- **Why j'ai, not je ai:** je ends in a vowel sound, and ai (from avoir) also starts with one — French doesn't allow two vowel sounds to collide like that, so je drops its e and takes an apostrophe instead: j'ai. This is the same elision pattern behind le/la becoming l', and is worth remembering as a general rule, not just for this one verb.
- **Where the "-s" ending actually goes:** a genuinely useful contrast for English speakers — English marks the *third* person singular with an -s (he/she plays), but French marks the *second* person singular instead (tu joues). It's easy to instinctively expect the French "marked" form to land on the same person as in English, and it doesn't.
- **Word check — conjugaison:** a student asked what "conjugation" means; the instructor defined it as everything related to how a verb's form changes — present tense, past tense, and so on.

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Ça va? vs Ça va. — question vs statement:** a student specifically asked whether "ça va" as a question and "ça va" as an answer are the same words. They are — the *only* difference is the question mark/intonation. Literally translated, ça va means "it is fine"; said as a question it becomes "is it going well? / how's it going?", and said as a statement it means "I'm fine / it's fine". The instructor used this to make a broader point: what turns a phrase into a question in speech is tone, not different wording.
- **Comment ça va = ça va:** confirmed as fully interchangeable — comment just adds the literal word "how", but the meaning and use are the same.
- **Salut is not just "bye":** salut means hello/hi. It can also be used to say bye, but only because of context — in the sample dialogue, the same word salut opens the conversation (hi) and closes it (bye). The instructor's point: don't memorize salut as meaning "bye" — memorize it as a casual greeting that works at both ends of a conversation.
- **de l'année — another elision:** "de la année" would collide two vowel sounds (a + a), so la drops its a and becomes l': de l'année. Directly parallels the je → j' pattern from Class 2 — French consistently avoids stacking vowel sounds this way.
- **Août's silent letters:** the accent circonflexe on the û in août marks another historically-dropped letter (like hôpital's story in Class 1). In modern spoken French the word is essentially just "oo" — the a and t are not pronounced.

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **quel vs comment — the difference students kept tripping on:** a student asked why "quel âge as-tu?" uses quel and not comment, since both can translate loosely to "how old are you?" The instructor's answer: comment means "how" and can't be swapped in here — quel âge (literally "what age") is simply the fixed way French asks the question, and you can't always translate word-for-word between the two languages.
- **quel vs quels — singular vs plural:** a student directly asked whether it's "quel" or "quels" and what decides it. Confirmed: quel is singular (asking about one thing, e.g. "what is the first day of the week"), quels is plural (asking about several, e.g. "what are the seven days of the week") — this is what grammarians call a relative pronoun (le pronom relatif) agreeing in number with what's being asked about.
- **j'ai 20 ans — why "have" not "am":** flagged as a clean example of why literal word-for-word translation breaks down between English and French — English says "I am 20 years old" (to be), French says "j'ai 20 ans" (to have). The instructor used this to reinforce a running theme: don't translate word by word, learn the French sentence pattern directly.
- **Counting 0–10 called "non-negotiable":** the instructor was explicit that knowing 0–10 instantly, without hesitation, is a hard baseline expectation — compared to knowing your own name. 11–31 builds on top of that base.
- **The pattern behind 11–16 vs 17–19:** the numbers 11–16 (onze–seize) are their own distinct words, but from 17 onward the pattern becomes visibly "10 + digit" (dix-sept = 10+7, dix-huit = 10+8, dix-neuf = 10+9) — the instructor pointed this out as a memory shortcut rather than 31 words to memorize individually.
- **Why 21, 31 use "et" but 22, 23 don't:** vingt et un (21), trente et un (31) insert "et" (and) before un, but vingt-deux (22), vingt-trois (23) don't — the "et" only shows up specifically before un in these decades.
- **Liaison with numbers, worked live:** un euro is pronounced as one linked word ("un-neuro") because of the vowel starting "euro"; the class contrasted this with un stylo (pen), where no liaison happens because "stylo" starts with a consonant. A student explicitly asked to confirm the rule was about the vowel triggering the link, not something else — confirmed yes.
- **s'il te plaît vs s'il vous plaît, and a side note on pronoun types:** a student asked about "te" vs "tu" — the instructor flagged that te is a different category of pronoun from the personal pronouns (je/tu/il...) and promised to explain pronoun types in more depth later, without fully resolving it in this class.
- **"..." (ellipsis) in a fill-in-the-blank phrase:** comment on dit ... en français? uses three dots to show a missing word slot — students practiced by inserting a specific word into that blank (e.g. comment on dit stylo en français?).

## Class 5 — Thursday, July 2, 2026

*Definite articles (le/la/l'/les), indefinite articles (un/une/des), and partitive articles (du/de la/de l'/des).*

### Grammar: Definite Articles (le / la / l' / les)

- Le, la, l' = "the" in English.
- LE is used for masculine nouns.
- LA is used for feminine nouns.
- L' is used before nouns starting with a vowel, including a mute "h".
- LES is used for the plural of masculine or feminine nouns.

### Examples: Definite articles

| Masculine | Feminine | Plural (either) |
|---|---|---|
| le village — the village | la ville — the city | les villages |
| le pont — the bridge | la région — the region | les villes |
| l'homme — the man | l'amie — girlfriend | les amis |
| l'ami — the friend |  | les amies |

### Grammar: Indefinite Articles (un / une / des)

- Un, une = "a / an" in English.
- UN is used for masculine nouns.
- UNE is used for feminine nouns.
- DES is used for the plural of masculine or feminine nouns.

### Examples: Indefinite articles

| Masculine | Feminine | Plural (either) |
|---|---|---|
| un village — a village | une ville — a city | des villages |
| un pont — a bridge | une région — a region | des villes |
| un homme — a man | une amie — a girlfriend | des amis |
| un ami — a friend |  | des amies |

### Grammar: Partitive Articles (some / any)

- Used for an unspecified quantity of something — "some" in English.
- de + le → du
- de + l' → de l'
- de + la → de la
- de + les → des

- Je voudrais du pain. — I would like some bread.
- Elle mange du chocolat. — She eats (some) chocolate.
- Nous buvons de l'eau minérale. — We drink (some) mineral water.
- Il achète de la viande. — He is buying (some) meat.
- Elle fait pousser des haricots verts. — She grows green beans.

---

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Why la ville and la région are feminine, but le village is masculine — no clean rule offered:** a student pushed directly on this ("how did village become masculine and city/region became feminine?") and the instructor's honest answer was that grammatical gender mostly has to be memorized, though word endings can hint at it (e.g. words ending in -age tend to be masculine) — the exceptions still have to be learned by heart.
- **ami vs amie — the single-letter gender marker:** ami (no e) = male friend, amie (with e) = female friend. A student asked whether this "add an E for feminine" pattern generalizes to other words — the instructor was careful to say no, this specific pattern is true for this word, not a blanket rule for all French nouns.
- **Telling male vs female "friend" apart in speech:** since ami/amie sound identical when spoken, the only way to know which is meant is context — e.g. if you already know the friend being discussed by name and know that person's gender.
- **le/la before a vowel always becomes l':** worked through explicitly with homme (l'homme) and ami (l'ami) — when le or la would sit directly before a word starting with a vowel or silent h, the yowel drops and an apostrophe takes its place, purely to avoid two vowel sounds colliding.
- **Partitive articles — the logic, not just the table:** the instructor stressed the partitive (du/de la/de l'/des) exists specifically for when you don't know or care about an exact quantity — "I want some bread" vs "I want the bread." A student connected the dots themselves mid-class, correctly guessing that pain and chocolat are both masculine (hence du), which the instructor confirmed.
- **Word order surprise — adjectives after the noun:** a student asked why "des haricots verts" (green beans) puts the color after the noun instead of before, as in English. The instructor confirmed French generally places descriptive adjectives after the noun, unlike English, and flagged this as its own grammar topic to cover properly later rather than explaining it fully here.

### Grammar: Liaison (linking sounds)

- When a word ending in a normally-silent consonant is followed by a word starting with a vowel (or mute h), the consonant is pronounced to link the two words smoothly. This is called liaison.
- un Américain — the "n" of "un" links to "Américain".
- les Allemands — the "s" of "les" links as a "z" sound into "Allemands".
- les amis — liaison happens ("lez-ami") since "amis" starts with a vowel.
- les villages — no liaison, since "village" starts with a consonant (v).

### Grammar: The three verb groups & First Group (ER) — present tense

- French verbs fall into three groups: First group (infinitive ends in -ER), Second group (-IR), Third group (-RE, -OIR, -DRE).
- For first-group verbs at the present tense, replace the -ER ending with: -e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent.

| habiter (to live) | parler (to speak) |
|---|---|
| j'habite | je parle |
| tu habites | tu parles |
| il/elle habite | il/elle parle |
| nous habitons | nous parlons |
| vous habitez | vous parlez |
| ils/elles habitent | ils/elles parlent |

- All first-group present-tense forms sound identical for je/tu/il-elle and ils/elles — the endings are silent.
- French always adds "s" to the second-person singular (tu manges), the same way English adds "s" to third-person singular (he/she eats) — it's just a different person that gets the "s".
- Aller (to go) looks like a first-group verb (ends in -er) but is actually irregular — the one big exception.

### Numbers: 70–99 (the tricky ones)

- French builds 70–99 out of 60 and 80 (there's no separate word for seventy, eighty, ninety).

### Grammar: jamais + negation (ne...pas / ne...jamais)

- Standard negation in French is formed with ne...pas (verb sandwiched between them).
- Je veux ce stylo. → Je ne veux pas ce stylo. (I want this pen → I don't want this pen.)
- Jamais is only used in the negative form — it replaces "pas" in the same sandwich structure (ne...jamais).
- Je ne veux jamais ce stylo. — I never want this pen.
- Je ne vais jamais au théâtre. — I never go to the theater.

### Grammar: pour + infinitive (detail)

- Whenever pour is followed by a verb, that verb is automatically in the infinitive (unconjugated) form.
- In English, "for" is often followed by "-ing" (for studying), but in French it maps to "to + verb" (pour étudier = to study / for studying).

### Grammar: Present Continuous → "en train de"

- French has no direct equivalent of English "-ing" (present continuous). Two options:
- 1) Just use the present tense: J'étudie. = I study / I am studying.
- 2) Use "être en train de + infinitive" for extra emphasis on an action in progress: Je suis en train d'étudier. = I am (in the middle of) studying.
- Both are correct — the second is more precise/emphatic.

| French | English |
|---|---|
| je suis en train d'étudier | I'm studying |
| je suis en train de laver | I'm washing |
| je suis en train de manger | I'm eating |

### Grammar: Recognizing verb groups by infinitive ending

| Ending | Group |
|---|---|
| -er | 1st group (e.g. laver — to wash) |
| -ir | 2nd group (e.g. finir — to finish) |
| -re / -oir | 3rd group (e.g. prendre — to take) |

- The infinitive is the base/dictionary form of a verb, before it's conjugated for a subject (je, tu, il...).

### Grammar: Second Group (IR) verbs — the two conditions

- Condition 1: the verb's infinitive ends in -IR.
- Condition 2: its present participle ends in -ISSANT.
- finir (to finish) → finissant (finishing)
- choisir (to choose) → choisissant (choosing)

| finir (to finish) | choisir (to choose) |
|---|---|

### Grammar: The present participle rule (-ANT vs -ISSANT)

- The present participle of almost every French verb ends in -ANT (1st group and 3rd group alike).
- The only exception is second-group (-IR / -ISSANT) verbs — their present participle ends in -ISSANT instead. This is actually how you can double-check a verb is second group.

| Verb | Group | Present participle |
|---|---|---|
| aller — to go | irregular (3rd) | en allant |
| manger — to eat | 1st | en mangeant |
| parler — to speak | 1st | en parlant |
| marcher — to walk | 1st | en marchant |
| partir — to leave | 3rd | partant |
| finir — to finish | 2nd | finissant |
| choisir — to choose | 2nd | choisissant |

### Grammar: Expressing an ongoing action

- French has no direct "-ing" present continuous tense. Two ways to express it:
- 1) être en train de + infinitive — emphasizes the action is happening right now.
- 2) Simply use the present tense — often just as natural.

- I'm going to school — je suis en train d'aller à l'école, or simply je vais à l'école.
- She is finishing her homework — elle est en train de finir son devoir, or simply elle finit son devoir.

### Grammar: Gérondif — en + present participle

- Gérondif = en + present participle (either -ANT or -ISSANT ending) = "while / by doing something".

- En marchant j'ai vu Karthik. — While walking, I saw Karthik.
- En choisissant j'étais confus. — While choosing, I got confused.

### Grammar: Present continuous — recap

- Je suis en train de travailler. / Je travaille. — Both mean "I'm working".

### Grammar: Future tense — First & Second group

- Add these endings straight onto the infinitive: -ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont.

| Pronoun | manger (1st group) | finir (2nd group) |
|---|---|---|
| je | mangerai | finirai |
| tu | mangeras | finiras |
| il / elle | mangera | finira |
| nous | mangerons | finirons |
| vous | mangerez | finirez |
| ils / elles | mangeront | finiront |

### Grammar: Future tense — Third group ending in -DRE or -RE

- Drop the final "e" from the infinitive, then add the same endings (-ai/-as/-a/-ons/-ez/-ont).

| Pronoun | vendre (to sell) | perdre (to lose) | mettre (to put) |
|---|---|---|---|
| je | vendrai | perdrai | mettrai |
| tu | vendras | perdras | mettras |
| il / elle | vendra | perdra | mettra |
| nous | vendrons | perdrons | mettrons |
| vous | vendrez | perdrez | mettrez |
| ils / elles | vendront | perdront | mettront |

### Grammar: Future tense — Être (irregular)

| Pronoun | Conjugation |
|---|---|
| je | serai |
| tu | seras |
| il / elle | sera |
| nous | serons |
| vous | serez |
| ils / elles | seront |

### Grammar: Future tense — Third group ending in -OIR (irregular stems)

- devoir → stem devr-, vouloir → stem voudr-, pouvoir → stem pourr-, then the same endings.

| Pronoun | devoir (have to) | vouloir (to want) | pouvoir (can) |
|---|---|---|---|
| je | devrai | voudrai | pourrai |
| tu | devras | voudras | pourras |
| il / elle | devra | voudra | pourra |
| nous | devrons | voudrons | pourrons |
| vous | devrez | voudrez | pourrez |
| ils / elles | devront | voudront | pourront |

### Grammar: Future tense — Third group ending in -IR

- partir and sortir just add the endings onto the full infinitive. venir is irregular — its stem is viendr-, not venir-.

| Pronoun | partir (to leave) | sortir (to go out) | venir (to come) |
|---|---|---|---|
| je | partirai | sortirai | viendrai |
| tu | partiras | sortiras | viendras |
| il / elle | partira | sortira | viendra |
| nous | partirons | sortirons | viendrons |
| vous | partirez | sortirez | viendrez |
| ils / elles | partiront | sortiront | viendront |

### Grammar: Future tense — Aller (fully irregular)

- Aller doesn't follow any of the patterns above — its future stem is simply "ir-".

| Pronoun | Conjugation |
|---|---|
| j' | irai |
| tu | iras |
| il / elle | ira |
| nous | irons |
| vous | irez |
| ils / elles | iront |

### Grammar: Conditional tense (le conditionnel)

- The conditional uses the exact same stem as the future tense, but different endings: -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient.

| Pronoun | manger (1st group) | choisir (2nd group) |
|---|---|---|
| je | mangerais — I would eat | choisirais — I would choose |
| tu | mangerais — you would eat | choisirais — you would choose |
| il / elle | mangerait — would eat | choisirait — would choose |
| nous | mangerions — we would eat | choisirions — we would choose |
| vous | mangeriez — you would eat | choisiriez — you would choose |
| ils / elles | mangeraient — they would eat | choisiraient — they would choose |

### Grammar: Should & Could

- "Should" = devoir in the conditional. "Could" = pouvoir in the conditional — same future stem (devr-/pourr-) plus the conditional endings.
- je devrais manger — I should eat.
- je pourrais manger — I could eat.

### Grammar: The Self-Introduction Structure

- Recommended order for a spoken self-introduction (greeting → name → nationality come first; the rest can flow in any order): greeting, name, nationality, age, date & place of birth, where you live, languages spoken, marital status, who you live with, profession, hobbies.

### Vocabulary: Three Ways to Give Your Name

- Je m'appelle Arti. — My name is Arti.
- Mon nom est Arti. — My name is Arti.
- Je suis Arti. — I am Arti.

### Grammar: Nationality & Profession — Always Feminine Agreement

- Quelle est votre nationalité ? (formal) / Quelle est ta nationalité ? (informal) — "nationalité" is a feminine noun, so the answer always takes the feminine adjective form regardless of the speaker's own gender: je suis indienne is correct even said by a man, because you're agreeing with "nationalité", not with yourself.
- Same logic for profession: quelle est votre profession ? uses quelle, not quel, because "profession" is feminine.

### Vocabulary: Marital Status (le statut marital)

| French | English |
|---|---|
| célibataire | single |
| marié / mariée | married |
| divorcé / divorcée | divorced |
| veuf / veuve | widowed |
| séparé / séparée | separated |
| fiancé / fiancée | engaged |

- Quel est votre statut marital ? — What is your marital status? (formal)
- Quel est ton statut marital ? — What is your marital status? (informal)

### Grammar: Asking Questions by Inversion

- Swapping the verb before the subject turns a statement into a question without needing a rising tone — the word order itself signals it's a question. Compare: tu habites (statement) vs habites-tu ? (question). With pronoun subjects, a linking -t- is inserted for pronunciation when the verb ends in a vowel: où habite-t-il ? With a named subject the verb stays as is: où habite Sri ?
- Où habitez-vous ? — Where do you live? (formal)
- Où habites-tu ? — Where do you live? (informal)
- Où habitons-nous ? — Where do we live?
- Combien de langues parlez-vous ? — How many languages do you speak?

### Grammar: Hobbies — Noun Form vs Verb Form

- Both forms are correct and mean essentially the same thing for A1 — this isn't a level-dependent distinction. Use whichever feels natural.
- J'aime la danse. — I like dancing. (noun form)
- J'aime danser. — I like to dance. (verb form)
- J'aime le cinéma. — I like going to the movies.
- J'aime regarder des films. — I like watching movies.

### Note: Names That Don't Translate

- Some language and place names carry a French equivalent (allemand for German, chinois for Chinese), but many don't — Indian regional languages like Hindi and Tamil, and place names like Ahmedabad, are simply said as-is in French. Examiners are tolerant of this; there's no need to force a translation that doesn't exist.

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Why the class opened with reassurance about mistakes, ten days out from the A1 exam:** several students admitted they didn't feel ready. The instructor's response was direct: no one ever feels fully ready before an exam — the point of this class is to make mistakes now, in a room with no stakes, precisely so the exam itself isn't the first time you're tested.
- **"Does the subject always come after the verb in a question?" — a student pushed on this directly, and the instructor drew a clear line:** both tu es là ? (statement + rising tone) and es-tu là ? (inversion) are valid questions in speech. But inversion is what lets a question be recognized by word order alone, without relying on tone — which is why it shows up constantly in formal written French.
- **Why there's no linking "-t-" in some inverted questions but there is in others — a student noticed the inconsistency and asked directly:** the instructor clarified it's about pronunciation, not grammar. A named subject like où habite Sri ? needs nothing extra, but a pronoun subject where the verb ends in a vowel (habite-t-il ?) gets a linking -t- inserted purely so the two vowel sounds don't collide.
- **The present continuous "doesn't exist" in French — repeated more forcefully than in earlier classes, in response to a student trying to force an English structure onto French:** a student asked whether où habitons-nous should use être en train de since "living" feels ongoing. The instructor's answer: no — plain present tense already covers ongoing action in French; forcing en train de here doesn't just sound unnatural, it's the wrong tool, and trying to translate English tense-for-tense into French was flagged as a recurring trap to actively unlearn.
- **Do Indian language and place names get "translated" into French, the way Chinese becomes chinois? — a student asked this directly, worried about being marked wrong:** the instructor's answer was reassuring: some European language names do have French equivalents (allemand for German), but Hindi, Tamil, and place names like Ahmedabad are simply said as-is — there's no invented French version to memorize, and examiners are tolerant of real names that don't translate.
- **"J'aime la danse" vs "j'aime danser" — a student pressed hard on whether these mean different things, and the instructor used a concrete contrast to settle it:** liking "la danse" (the noun) doesn't necessarily mean you enjoy dancing yourself — you could like watching someone else's dance performance. Liking "danser" (the verb) specifically means you enjoy the act of dancing. In casual hobby-listing context, though, both are treated as interchangeable and neither is wrong for A1.
- **Is "le cinéma" as a hobby the same as "regarder un film"? — a genuinely extended back-and-forth, because a student worried they meant different activities:** the instructor clarified "le cinéma" implicitly means going out to a movie theater to watch something, while "regarder un film" is more neutral about location (home or theater) — but for the purpose of listing a hobby, either is understood and sufficient without needing to spell out further detail.
- **A live, good-natured correction that became the best teaching moment of the class:** a student (Kartik) tried to say "I married the love of my life in December 2015" and it came out garbled mid-recording. Talking it through live, the class worked out the correct form together — j'ai épousé l'amour de ma vie — using it as a real example of passé composé (introduced here as roughly the English present perfect), deliberately distinguished from simply saying je suis marié. The instructor noted, genuinely, that the class also taught him something that day — a student's use of "thriller" to mean a suspense film, which he hadn't been sure translated the same way in French.
- **Closing note on why the instructor wanted students' practice recordings shared with the group, not just submitted privately:** several students hesitated to have their audio/video posted. The instructor's case: seeing classmates visibly nervous but still trying is more motivating for the group than a polished performance would be — the goal was reframed from "avoid embarrassment" to "normalize trying," especially for students who'd skipped the session out of nerves.
