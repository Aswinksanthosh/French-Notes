### Grammar: Demonstrative Adjectives (les adjectifs démonstratifs)

- Used to say "this/that/these/those." Like all French adjectives, they agree in gender and number with the noun.

| Form | Used for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ce | masculine singular | ce stylo — this pen |
| cet | masculine singular, noun starts with a vowel or (silent) "h" | cet ami — this friend; cet étudiant — this student; cet homme — this man; cet hôpital — this hospital |
| cette | feminine singular | cette voiture — this car; cette femme — this woman; cette fille — this girl |
| ces | plural (masc. or fem.) | ces stylos — these pens; ces valises — these suitcases |

- "Cet" exists purely for pronunciation — it links smoothly with a following vowel/h sound. Context tells you gender when the pronunciation is identical (e.g. "cet ami" for a male friend vs "cette amie" for a female friend sound similar spoken).

### Grammar: c'est vs il/elle est

| Form | Meaning | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| c'est | it is / this is | followed by an article + noun (un/une/le/la + noun) |
| il est / elle est | he is / she is | followed directly by an adjective or nationality |

- C'est un artiste. — This is an artist.
- C'est une visite d'1 heure. — This is a one-hour visit.
- C'est le quartier historique. — This is the historical neighborhood.
- C'est la place du Capitole. — This is the Capitol place.
- Il est américain. — He is American.
- Elle est calme. — She is calm.

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Why the 39-verb ER list needs to be memorized, not just recognized — explained via an English parallel:** a student asked directly whether they really need to memorize the whole list. The instructor compared it to English irregular past tenses (go → went/gone) that just have to be learned outright — these are common verbs students will encounter constantly on the exam, so recognition alone isn't enough; active recall matters.
- **cet vs cette — the exact same masculine-vowel-exception logic from possessive adjectives, reapplied to demonstratives:** a student asked directly whether cette also has a special vowel-triggered form the way cet does for masculine nouns. Confirmed: no — cette is used for ALL feminine singular nouns regardless of whether they start with a vowel; the vowel-linking exception only exists on the masculine side (ce → cet), because cette already ends in a vowel sound and doesn't need the fix.
- **cet ami vs cette amie — a student asked how you'd ever tell these apart by ear in a real listening exercise:** the instructor's honest answer: you often can't, from sound alone — it comes down to context (who's being discussed, established earlier in the conversation), the same resolution given for son/sa ambiguity back in Class 9.
- **c'est vs il/elle est — introduced for the first time here, with a live contrast built sentence by sentence:** c'est un artiste ("this is an artist," using an indefinite article + noun) vs il est américain ("he is American," adjective directly, no article). A student asked whether the rule generalizes reliably — instructor confirmed the pattern: c'est pairs with an article+noun, il/elle est pairs with a bare adjective or nationality.
- **Grenoblois vs "il est de Grenoble" — two valid ways to say someone's from a place, and when each fits:** a student asked whether these are fully interchangeable. Instructor's answer: yes, but with a subtlety — a demonym like Grenoblois (built from the city name, similar to "Bengalurean") implies more of an identity/origin claim, whereas "il est de Grenoble" is a plainer statement of current or past location, comparable to the American/from America distinction discussed in the same breath.
- **Plural exceptions flagged but deliberately deferred:** a student asked whether French plurals always just add S (as in English) — the instructor confirmed there are exceptions (e.g., words ending in -eau take -x, not -s, like tableau → tableaux) but declined to go deep into it here, promising to return to it as a dedicated topic and asking the class to remind them later.
