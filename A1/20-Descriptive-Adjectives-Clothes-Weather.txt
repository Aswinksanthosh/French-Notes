*Montrer & descriptive adjectives (léger/lourd/rond), clothes, accessories, materials, weather, technology, and everyday objects.*

### Vocabulary: Montrer (to show) and related words

- une montre — a watch (the wristwatch, noun) — not to be confused with montrer.
- montrer — to show (verb) — a false-friend trap: it does NOT mean "to watch".
- regarder — to watch / to look at.
- porter — to carry / to wear.
- le nœud — bow tie. la cravate — tie.

### Grammar: Léger/lourd/rond — descriptive adjectives

| English | Masculine | Feminine |
|---|---|---|
| light | léger | légère |
| heavy | lourd | lourde |
| round | rond | ronde |

- Le porte-clé est rond. — The keychain is round.

### Grammar: Montrer — future vs present (recap)

- Je montrerai ma montre. — I will show my watch. (future)
- Je montre ma montre. — I'm showing my watch. (present)

- montrer is a regular first-group (ER) verb: je montre, tu montres...

### Classroom Discussion & Q&A

- **Why the class started with a full mock self-introduction exercise, in students' own words first:** the instructor asked students to draft self-introductions in English first, then translate structure (not word-for-word) into French, explicitly modeling the sequence: greeting → name → nationality → age → date of birth → place of birth → current residence → languages spoken → marital status → who you live with → profession/hobbies. Students were told order is flexible except greeting-name-nationality, which should come first.
- **A student asked whether family details need to go deep (e.g., describing each parent's job) — the instructor said no, deliberately keeping it simple:** explicitly told students not to over-elaborate this early — "I live with my parents" or "I'm single" is sufficient; the goal at this stage is speaking confidence, not exhaustive detail.
- **A genuinely emotional side-conversation about repeat exam attempts:** a new/returning student (Antara) shared she hadn't passed on a first attempt elsewhere and was nervous; the instructor and classmates responded with encouragement, and the instructor used it to reinforce that speaking practice via recorded audio/video (not just silent mirror practice) is what actually builds real exam confidence, since no one grades a private mirror session.
- **montre (noun, "watch") vs montrer (verb, "to show") — a live confusion caught and corrected on the spot:** a student conjugated montrer and initially wrote "je montre" thinking of the noun "watch"; the instructor used this exact mix-up as a teaching moment — same root spelling, completely different function (object vs. verb), resolved only by context, similar to the porter (to wear/carry) ambiguity from earlier classes.
- **Regional accent question — French from France vs. Ivory Coast vs. Canadian French, addressed directly:** a student asked whether the instructor's French (Ivorian) differs from France's or Canada's the way American and British English differ. The instructor's answer: yes, real accent and some vocabulary differences exist between regions (citing a specific example: how "baguette" or certain everyday words can differ colloquially in Canadian French), but mutual intelligibility holds as long as speakers articulate clearly, especially when talking to someone from a different French-speaking region.
- **léger/légère and lourd/lourde — the standard masculine/feminine adjective pattern, but flagged for a silent-letter trap:** léger (light, masculine) drops the final r sound; the feminine légère adds the r sound back audibly. Lourd (heavy, masculine) has a silent d; lourde (feminine) pronounces the d. A student asked to confirm the general "feminine adjectives often end in a pronounced consonant that's silent in the masculine" pattern — confirmed as a recognizable trend, not an absolute rule.
- **A true/false reading-comprehension exercise about a French clothing-size chart, worked through as a group — and a student caught an error in real time:** students read a passage stating someone's waist size and had to evaluate a matching true/false statement against a size conversion table. A student flagged that the instructor's initial answer didn't match the table's actual data, prompting a joint re-check — the instructor acknowledged the mistake and confirmed the corrected answer was false, modeling that even the instructor double-checks against source data rather than asserting from memory.
